In plain language

The Navier–Stokes equations describe how fluids such as water and air move. One of mathematics' best-known open questions asks whether a smooth, freely evolving flow can ever spin up without limit in a finite time. No computer simulation can settle that question, because every simulation chops the fluid into a finite grid. A simulation can, however, show which of its own results can be trusted and which cannot.

SpectraNS-3D does exactly that. We started two counter-rotating tubes of swirling fluid, nudged them toward each other, and let them interact with no outside push. We then ran the same flow on three increasingly fine grids (512³, 640³ and 768³ points) and asked a simple question: which answers stay the same when the grid gets finer?

  • Big-picture quantities are trustworthy. The total energy and the total amount of swirl (enstrophy) agree between the two finest grids to within about 0.01%.
  • The sharpest local spin is not yet trustworthy. The single strongest spot of swirl still shifts by 1–1.5% when the grid is refined.
  • Two agreeing grids can fool you. On the first two grids, that peak agreed to 0.009%, which looked converged. The third grid moved it by 1.1%. Checking each quantity separately, rather than trusting one passing test, is the main lesson.

Two smaller pieces of work build on that lesson. A four-qubit run on IBM's ibm_kingston quantum computer repeated the question with hardware noise in place of grid resolution. Again the big-picture answer survived and the sharpest local value did not, this time even moving in the wrong direction. A separate audit checked that OpenAI's computer-verified Navier–Stokes theorems state the official Clay problem faithfully; it found no mismatch.

Everything needed to check this is included: the solver, 239 automated tests for the simulations plus 31 for the analyses, the hash-verified evidence and a script that recomputes the comparisons on an ordinary laptop. The finest run used one rented NVIDIA H200 GPU for about 95 minutes, at a cost of about USD 7.

What this release does not claim

This is a finite-interval numerical study through simulated time t = 1.10. It does not prove that the flow stays smooth, does not show a singularity (blowup), and does not reach the point where the total swirl stops growing. It does not reproduce, prove or disprove any analytical Navier–Stokes result. OpenAI's September 2026 result concerns a different, externally forced construction; this release audits only whether its Lean theorem statements match Clay's problem, not whether its proofs are correct. The quantum run demonstrates no quantum advantage: a laptop computes its exact answer.

Technical summary

Unforced incompressible Navier–Stokes on the 2π-periodic cube, viscosity ν = 5×10⁻⁴, with an anti-parallel pair of sinusoidally perturbed Gaussian vortex tubes (Γ = 5, core a = 0.25, separation 0.6, perturbation 0.35). The solver is Fourier pseudo-spectral in FP64, with solenoidal projection, a strict spherical cutoff |k| < N/3 and audited low-storage RK4 (Carpenter–Kennedy). Diagnostics, enstrophy budgets and full shell spectra are recorded every 0.01 time units.

New in v1.1.0: why integrals agree and local peaks do not

A post-hoc analysis of the published, hash-verified spectra locates the difference in wavenumber space. At t = 1.00, under 1% of enstrophy but about 24% of viscous enstrophy loss and 33% of a peak-vorticity proxy sit in shells where the N640 and N768 spectra differ by more than 1%; all three shares peak at t = 0.99, where the local-peak mismatch is largest. The conventional analyticity-strip fit proved too sensitive to its fitting window to certify resolution and is reported as a negative result. No new simulation was run.

New in v1.2.0: a pre-registered quantum hardware run

One Fourier spectral advection step (QFT, modal phase, inverse QFT) on a 16-point periodic grid in four qubits (physical qubits 1–2–3–16), at four shifts and two measurement bases, using 13 s of QPU time. Four predictions were committed before submission and are reported as they came out:

PredictionResult
P1Integral-type centroid survivesPass: within 0.08 cell

P3Runs agree with each other more than with truthPass: pairwise TVD 0.027 vs 0.253 from exact
P4Known-answer twin calibrates the peak bias awayFail: correction overshot by up to 32%

The failures trace to physical qubit 1 misreading |0⟩ as |1⟩ 28% of the time during the job, plus residual asymmetric error. The fault was invisible in the calibration data and was caught by readout circuits included in the job. The finding is narrow: a local observable's error can change sign, and a one-parameter noise correction can make it worse when the error is structured.

New in v1.2.0: statement-fidelity audit of OpenAI's Lean theorems

The audit traces Clay's breakdown alternatives (C) and (D) through Google DeepMind's independent Formal Conjectures statement, OpenAI's challenge file and definitions, to the proved theorems, at pinned commits. It also checks the meaning of smoothness, Laplacian and gradient in the pinned Mathlib. (C) matches clause by clause. (D) matches Clay as amended by its official errata requiring a periodic pressure. The proofs themselves were not built or checked. The unforced alternatives (A) and (B) remain open.

Validation and reproducibility

  • 239 tests passed on the H200, plus eight CPU/GPU reference comparisons.
  • Eleven controller rehearsals, including seven deliberate process kills, verify what evidence survives failure.
  • All 132 protected evidence files are hash-verified, and an independent CPU script replays every comparison.
  • Exact source, configuration, runtime and package versions are bound to each run.
  • The quantum run's circuits, manifest, job record, counts and dry run are committed unedited, with 21 offline tests; the Lean audit script re-fetches six pinned files and verifies their SHA256 values.
